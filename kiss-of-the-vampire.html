The product & context

What is Kiss of the Vampire?

A 1963 film poster for the Hammer horror film Kiss of the Vampire (dir. Don Sharp). Produced by Hammer Film Productions and distributed by Rank and Universal. It is studied for Component 1, Section A — Media Language & Representation only (there is no Section B Industry/Audience study for this text).

Intended as the third film in the Dracula (1958) series, though the script never names Stoker's character — perhaps to avoid comparison with Christopher Lee, who did not appear.

Hammer & the 1960s

  • Hammer (founded 1934) is one of the world's oldest film companies and is synonymous with British horror — Dracula, The Curse of Frankenstein, The Mummy — enjoying worldwide distribution and success, partly through partnerships with US studios.
  • Historical context (1963): early Beatlemania, the JFK assassination, the "swinging sixties", the Soviet Union sending the first woman into space, and the Cuban Missile Crisis. Horror reflects societal fears.
  • Gender politics specifically: the 1960s marks the start of women's sexual liberation, helped by the contraceptive pill (introduced 1960). More women than ever were entering the paid workforce, and 1960s feminists were campaigning for equal pay, an end to sexual harassment, and greater equality with men — equal pay legislation passed in the US in 1963, the same year this poster was released.
  • This means two competing representations can be read into the poster at once: "older" stereotypes of women as passive victims of men, and more modern "male fears" of women challenging male dominance.

Media Language

Genre signifiers & codes

  • The poster packs in Gothic-vampire signifiers: vampire bats, a remote castle, a full moon and low-key / high-contrast chiaroscuro lighting.
  • The "wooden" font and the bloodied "fang" reflect vampire lore (feeding on blood; the stake as the means of destruction). Blood also reminds audiences Hammer had reinvented the genre.
  • The Eastman Color banner tells audiences the film is in vibrant saturated colour when many films were still black and white. The American spelling "Color" signals this is a poster for the lucrative US market.
  • The Universal logo marks the US distributor and gives cine-literate audiences a call-back to Universal's own horror franchises (Whale's Frankenstein, Lugosi's Dracula).
  • The artistic, non-photo-real key art reflects the technological limitations of the early 1960s — the "painted" style is conventional of the period and echoes the poster for Christopher Lee's Dracula, even though this film moves the story on.
  • Star billing: conventionally, the higher-paid male actors are listed first and in order of fame — Clifford Evans is credited first, having starred in Hammer's 1961 hit Curse of the Werewolf. This anchors audience expectations before they've even seen the image.
  • Applying Barthes' five codes precisely: the hermeneutic code is the enigma of the relationship between the male and female vampire (emphasised by the word "kiss") and the fate of their two victims; the semantic code is carried by the bats (conventional signifiers of vampirism); the symbolic code is the wider pattern of darkness, the moon and the male victim's submissive gesture, all reinforcing horror and fear.

Applying theory to ML

Steve Neale — genre: "genres are instances of repetition and difference". The familiar vampire iconography is the repetition; what the poster offers that is new is the difference.

Roland Barthes — the five codes (enigma, action, semantic, symbolic, referential) help unpack how signs create multiple meanings; the Universal logo is a referential code pointing to horror history.

Claude Lévi-Strauss — structuralism: texts are constructed through binary oppositions. Here that's the romantic connotation of "kiss" set against the monstrous "vampire" in the title itself, and more widely good/evil, human/monster, and safety/danger across the whole poster.

Applying Neale, the bats, castle, full moon and chiaroscuro lighting satisfy the audience's generic expectations of 1960s Gothic horror (repetition), while the Eastman Color banner and Hammer's saturated, "rock and roll" style provide the difference "essential to the economy of the genre". The Universal logo works as a Barthesian referential code, rewarding cine-literate spectators with a call-back to Frankenstein and Dracula.

Representation

Gender & genre

  • The poster features no protagonists — only vampire antagonists and sacrificial prey.
  • Dress codes: both women wear pale dresses in light material, reinforcing their femininity by highlighting the curves of their bodies and revealing the flesh of their upper chests and arms.
  • Gesture codes: the woman on the left is the stereotypical passive victim — the male vampire's power is emphasised by the fact he holds her by just one arm. The second woman is far more aggressive — baring her teeth, arm raised almost fist-like as she's bitten by the bat — while her male "victim" is submissive: on his knees, head back, throat exposed, represented in a non-stereotypically dominant way by comparison.
  • The male vampire himself seems uncharacteristically fearful, his arm thrown defensively across his body — perhaps protecting himself from the newly powerful female vampire.
  • The male vampire's one-handed grip demonstrates strength; the female victim's tight costume and exposed flesh offer male gaze pleasures and conform to sexist gender norms of the time.
  • But the female vampire is more powerful — sexualised yet central to the narrative, about to deliver the "vampire kiss" of the title. This is a binary opposite of the usual "woman as prey". The male victim is passive, throat exposed, effectively faceless — an "everyman" threatened by the newly empowered woman.
  • Reflecting the 1960s (women seeking greater equality — bell hooks), Hammer nods to female empowerment to attract female audiences, but frames feminism as a threat to patriarchal power. Genre shifts to reflect the social realities of when the film was made, not when it is set.

Theory you can apply

Stuart Hall — representation: the castle, bats, cape and dripping blood form part of the "shared conceptual road map" that gives meaning to the poster's world — the audience is actively encouraged to decode this familiar generic iconography and construct meaning from it, not simply absorb a single fixed message.

David Gauntlett — identity: perhaps the female vampire acts as a role model or identity resource for women struggling against male oppression, or those wanting to be seen as men's equals, whatever the narrative or setting.

Van Zoonen, bell hooks, plus Reception and Cultivation theory for historical vs contemporary audience response.

The female victim's tight costume and exposed flesh construct her through the male gaze, conforming to sexist norms (Van Zoonen). Yet the dominant female vampire inverts the usual "female as prey" convention — sexualised but central and powerful — reflecting 1960s anxieties about women's empowerment (bell hooks). Applying Hall, the audience actively decodes the poster's shared iconography (castle, bats, blood) to construct this meaning, while Gauntlett would argue the female vampire offers women a more assertive identity resource. Hammer thus uses genre to voice contemporary social change while exposing male fears, presenting feminism as a threat to patriarchal power.
