Product & contextThe set text at a glance

  • What: Assassin's Creed III: Liberation — part of the hugely successful Assassin's Creed franchise, developed by Ubisoft and released on 30 October 2012, the same day as the main Assassin's Creed III.
  • Platform: originally exclusive to the Sony PlayStation Vita handheld; re-released in 2014 as a modified HD version across PS3, Xbox 360 and Windows.
  • Hero: Aveline de Grandpré — the franchise's first playable female assassin, a mixed-race woman in 18th-century New Orleans. A strong female protagonist added to target a wider audience alongside familiar franchise pleasures (Assassins vs Templars, open-world play).
  • Studied for: Media Industries (production, distribution, circulation, technological change, marketing, regulation) and Audiences (targeting, categorisation, consumption, interpretation and interaction).

Framework · your focus 1 of 2Media Industries

Production — a multinational franchise

  • Ubisoft is a large multinational games company; big-studio production like this is typically multinational and highly collaborative (writer Jill Murray describes constant communication between writers and designers).
  • The game is part of an established franchise with a huge fan-base. Hesmondhalgh — cultural industries are risky, so producers minimise risk and maximise audiences by reusing a proven brand while adding novelty (a female lead, a new handheld platform).
  • "Software sells hardware": a strong franchise game is used to drive sales of the console — here, to push the new PS Vita.

Distribution & circulation

  • Simultaneous release with Assassin's Creed III (on PS3) aimed for maximum impact across the three major markets: Japan, North America and Europe.
  • Digital circulation: later versions were bought over the internet via Steam, the PlayStation Network and Xbox Live — a shift from physical to digital distribution.
  • Convergence: owning both the Vita and PS3 versions unlocked extra content; the Vita's Wi-Fi/3G enabled DLC (weapons, skins, characters) and multiplayer — deepening engagement and identification.

  • The PS Vita (8th-generation tech) was built with smartphone-like features (touchscreen, Wi-Fi, 3G, Bluetooth) to tap the boom in mobile gaming — bringing "AAA" production values to handheld play.
  • Video games are culturally and financially huge, with profits rivalling major films across Japan, Europe and the USA. New hardware or franchise releases can be major economic events.

Regulation

  • Until July 2012, UK games were rated by the BBFC; now the Video Standards Council (VSC) applies the PEGI system (Pan-European Game Information) — age ratings plus content descriptors, harmonising child protection across Europe. Liberation is rated PEGI 18 for violence.
  • Livingstone & Lunt — convergent, online-connected devices (like the Vita) create new opportunities and new risks that complicate regulation: should it be more flexible, should industries self-regulate, should individuals build media literacy and decide for themselves?
  • Curran & Seaton — power concentrated in a few big publishers like Ubisoft shapes what gets made.

Framework · your focus 2 of 2Audiences

Targeting & constructing the audience

  • Handheld = a more casual, mobile "on-the-go" gamer — a different target from the traditional console/PC player.
  • A female protagonist is unusual in the franchise and targets a different demographic, potentially offering a point of identification for female gamers (audience construction, not just targeting).
  • Franchise loyalty: existing fans are targeted with familiar pleasures (Assassins vs Templars, open world) — a pre-sold audience.
  • Categorisation: audiences are grouped by demographics (age, gender, income/status) and psychographics (e.g. the 4Cs), and reached via opinion leaders and marketing.

The changing "gamer" & Aveline's appeal

The old stereotype of the gamer as a "lone male teenager in their bedroom" is out of date — players are now all ages, genders and demographics. Aveline widens that appeal:

🗡️ Assassin

Full weapons & free-running — combat power and rooftop mobility.

👛 Lady

Respected society woman — guards protect her; can charm and bribe, but limited agility.

⛓️ Slave

Move unseen through crowds and work sites, but guards punish any misstep harshly.

This social-stealth system ties gameplay to gender and ethnicity, inspired by real figures like Harriet Tubman. Writer Jill Murray designed the personas so the player experiences the story. It offers a richer point of identification — supporting arguments (e.g. Keza MacDonald) that games need more women to broaden their audience.

Effects, moral panic & the active audience

  • The PEGI 18 rating (for violence) puts Liberation into the long-running media-effects debate. Bandura (the Bobo doll study, hypodermic-syringe model) argues violent media can be observed and imitated, with fears of desensitisation.
  • But games are interactive. Does the player actively choosing to act complicate Bandura's passive "imitation" model? Uses & Gratifications — audiences actively use games for challenge, escapism, identity and social play. Hall — audiences decode differently (preferred, negotiated, oppositional).
  • Counter-evidence: a Guardian summary of the research (Etchells) reports the link between games and aggression is weak — a study where players played GTA V, The Sims 3 or nothing for two months found no significant negative effects. This challenges the "video games cause violence" myth.

Interaction, fandom & participation

  • Jenkins — fandom: the AC franchise has a passionate online fan community that discusses, creates and can even influence the games' development. Games evolved alongside the internet and offer social, multiplayer experiences.
  • Downloadable content, multiplayer and cross-platform unlocks show the interrelationship between technology and patterns of consumption — audiences are active participants, not passive receivers.
