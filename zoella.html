Product & contextWho is Zoella?

  • Zoe Elizabeth Sugg, from Brighton, started her blog zoella.co.uk in 2009 aged 19; her YouTube channels grew to millions of subscribers, making her one of Britain's biggest content creators / influencers.
  • She is a best-selling author (the Girl Online novels), had her own Zoella Beauty and Zoella Lifestyle ranges, and was the first Digital Ambassador for Mind (the mental-health charity), speaking openly about her anxiety. Partner: fellow YouTuber Alfie Deyes ("Zalfie").
  • The set text asks you to study: the codes and conventions of the home page (layout & design); examples of posts (blogs/vlogs); and examples of interactivity (links to social & participatory media).
  • Cultural icon: a Cosmopolitan cover, a Madame Tussauds waxwork, and (with Alfie) the first non-music artists on a Band Aid single. Burgess & Green call such figures "YouTube stars" — homegrown brands making a living from advertising revenue and their own external websites.

Framework 1 of 4Media Language

Home page & channel — codes and conventions

  • Blog = a regularly updated site with posts in reverse-chronological order, made interactive by hyperlinks and a comments section. Beauty, fashion and lifestyle are the most popular genres because they share personal thoughts and experiences.
  • Home-page conventions: a customised banner & logo (Zoella's handwritten-style signature font), a clean grid of thumbnailed posts, navigation tabs (Style, Beauty, Lifestyle) and prominent links to Instagram, Twitter, Facebook and the shop.
  • Barthes — every sign carries meaning: soft pastel palette, rounded fonts and cosy imagery connote warmth, femininity and approachability.

Media Language · key skillVlogging conventions

How vloggers manufacture "authenticity"

In a polished, saturated media world, YouTubers build products that appear unmediated. The "amateur" look is a careful construction of authenticity:

  • Direct mode of address & single-camera confessional setup → an intimate, video-diary tone that builds parasocial interaction (the one-sided "friendship" the viewer feels).
  • Jump-cut editing gives raw energy; kept-in mistakes/retakes and handheld POV shots signal it is unscripted and real.
  • Bedroom setting & everyday mise-en-scène = private, relatable space; natural make-up/costume = "effortless" relatability; high-key lighting = a wholesome, innocent tone (which also reassures the parents watching over their children's viewing).
  • Zoella's honesty about her anxiety deepens the human connection — what Katz & Lazarsfeld would call personal identity.

Framework 2 of 4Representation

Self-representation & the "girl next door" brand

Vlogs let creators represent themselves without intermediaries — but the "unmediated" persona is still selected and constructed, which makes them gatekeepers of their own image. Zoella's carefully built brand identity is the "girl next door": friendly, approachable, "normal", "just like you" — an aspirational yet relatable female role model.

  • Gauntlett — identity: media give audiences tools and "stories" to construct their own sense of self; Zoella's shared experiences help fans make sense of their own lives.
  • Butler — gender performativity: gender is performed, not natural — a social construction taught and "policed" by external influences (Gender Trouble, 1990). Zoella largely reinforces conventional femininity through make-up, hair, costume and domestic content (e.g. "Getting Organised for Christmas"), and traditional binaries in the "Zalfie" relationship.
  • Hall — representation & the "burden of representation"; the near-absence of non-white participants can construct a sense of "otherness" by omission (ideas linked to Gilroy; Alvarado's stereotypes — exotic, dangerous, humorous, pitied).

Framework 3 of 4 · your priority areaMedia Industries & economic context

The big debate: has the internet "democratised" the media?

The heart of the industry debate is how far technological change has democratised the media by letting "ordinary" users create and distribute content without professional intermediaries.

  • Yes — disruption/democratisation: creators bypass the gatekeepers. All a vlogger needs is a cheap camera and an internet connection, producing an explosion of user-generated content. Production and distribution are "no longer the preserve of the professional elite" (Web 2.0). Shirky — the "end of the audience": consumers become prosumers who speak back.
  • No — reinforcement: the internet is dominated by a few powerful tech companies that monetise our personal data into advertising, and traditional media have migrated online, crowding out smaller providers. Hesmondhalgh — the radical potential of the internet is limited by large companies chasing profit and minimising risk.

The rise of YouTube & the economics of attention

  • Smartphones gave YouTube its key ingredient: an "always-on" culture — video anywhere, any time.
  • Dominic Smales (founder of Gleam Futures, Zoella's management agency) says the appeal of stars like Zoella to advertisers is the personal, parasocial connection with their audience — that gives them influence, and brands buy the "credibility" of association.
  • Large subscriber bases are lucrative because of the web traffic they generate — hence the rise of Multi-Channel Networks (MCNs): firms that curate thousands of channels and help creators with production, marketing and monetisation in return for a % of ad revenue.

Regulation of online media

  • Online media is harder to regulate than TV/radio: the sheer quantity of content, so many sources, its global nature, and no single body in control (part of its appeal is its lack of regulation).
  • Advertising in blogs/vlogs is subject to the ASA and the CAP Code: paid content (ads, advertorials, sponsored posts) must be signposted (e.g. #ad). Creators must also obey consumer-protection law (UK OFT; US FTC) — breaches can bring sanctions.
  • YouTube self-regulates via Community Guidelines and its Partner Policies, which push creators to make "advertiser-friendly" content or lose monetisation. Ofcom now has online-safety duties (2023).
  • Livingstone & Lunt — regulation must balance the interests of citizens (protection) and consumers (choice). Curran & Seaton — MCNs and platform concentration show power gathering in a few hands.

The "Adpocalypse" — economics shaping content

In the 2017 "Adpocalypse", brands pulled ads after they appeared beside extremist and offensive videos; Google/YouTube responded by using algorithms to demonetise "sensitive" content. This is a clear case of economic/commercial factors shaping media content — creators self-censor to stay advertiser-friendly (Zoella beeps others' swearing, avoids controversial topics, and never swears herself).

The revenue streams

▶ Advertising

The YouTube Partner Programme shares ad revenue with creators. Ad types: display/banner, overlay, and video ads (skippable/non-skippable pre-rolls; ≤6s bumper ads). Google AdSense targets ads by browsing history.

🤝 Sponsorship & brand deals

Paid to review, endorse or promote products. Influencer endorsement has a big effect on sales, so brands pay a set fee or per view/click.

🛍️ Merchandise & own ranges

Zoella Beauty (12 countries by 2016) & Zoella Lifestyle homeware in Boots, Superdrug, WHSmith, Dunelm & more — diversification that also builds brand recognition.

📚 Books & media

The Girl Online novels; magazine covers, TV & radio — extending the brand across traditional media.

💬 Platform features

Post-Adpocalypse extras: channel memberships, Super Chat / Super Stickers and merchandise shelves built into YouTube.

🎤 Appearances & events

VidCon, Summer in the City, Amity Fest (Gleam Futures), meet-and-greets and red-carpet appearances that also market the brand.

Key economic point: creators increasingly earn outside the platform (products, books, appearances) the way traditional celebrities do. Zoella said her licensed beauty/lifestyle range was "by far the project that earns me the most money out of anything I do" — before choosing not to renew the deal (ranges discontinued 2020).

Framework 4 of 4Audiences

Targeting, reaching & the active audience

  • Targeted through content appeal (beauty, fashion, baking, "how-to" videos, a personal mode of address) and through marketing/circulation: collaboration videos (cross-subscribe "call to action" = synergy), conventions (VidCon, Summer in the City, Amity Fest), and red-carpet/mainstream-media appearances.
  • Interaction & participation: likes, comments and requests mean fans directly influence content. Shirky — audiences "speak back"; Jenkins — participatory culture, "textual poaching" and spreadable media; Gauntlett — YouTube is where "conversations and connections" develop. But Buckingham warns real "participation" (making videos) differs from posting the odd comment.
  • Gerbner — cultivation & resonance: young people now spend more time online than watching TV (Ofcom 2016, 5–15s), so their view of reality is increasingly cultivated by vloggers like Zoella — especially where content resonates with their own lives.
