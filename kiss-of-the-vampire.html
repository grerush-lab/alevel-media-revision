The product & context

What is Kiss of the Vampire?

A 1963 film poster for the Hammer horror film Kiss of the Vampire (dir. Don Sharp). Produced by Hammer Film Productions and distributed by Rank and Universal. It is studied for Component 1, Section A — Media Language & Representation only (there is no Section B Industry/Audience study for this text).

Intended as the third film in the Dracula (1958) series, though the script never names Stoker's character — perhaps to avoid comparison with Christopher Lee, who did not appear.

Hammer & the 1960s

  • Hammer (founded 1934) is one of the world's oldest film companies and is synonymous with British horror — Dracula, The Curse of Frankenstein, The Mummy — enjoying worldwide distribution and success, partly through partnerships with US studios.
  • Historical context (1963): early Beatlemania, the JFK assassination, the "swinging sixties" and sexual revolution (the contraceptive pill), the Soviet Union sending the first woman into space, and the Cuban Missile Crisis. Horror reflects societal fears.

Media Language

Genre signifiers & codes

  • The poster packs in Gothic-vampire signifiers: vampire bats, a remote castle, a full moon and low-key / high-contrast chiaroscuro lighting.
  • The "wooden" font and the bloodied "fang" reflect vampire lore (feeding on blood; the stake as the means of destruction). Blood also reminds audiences Hammer had reinvented the genre.
  • The Eastman Color banner tells audiences the film is in vibrant saturated colour when many films were still black and white. The American spelling "Color" signals this is a poster for the lucrative US market.
  • The Universal logo marks the US distributor and gives cine-literate audiences a call-back to Universal's own horror franchises (Whale's Frankenstein, Lugosi's Dracula).
  • The artistic, non-photo-real key art reflects the technological limitations of the early 1960s.

Applying theory to ML

Steve Neale — genre: "genres are instances of repetition and difference". The familiar vampire iconography is the repetition; what the poster offers that is new is the difference.

Roland Barthes — the five codes (enigma, action, semantic, symbolic, referential) help unpack how signs create multiple meanings; the Universal logo is a referential code pointing to horror history.

Applying Neale, the bats, castle, full moon and chiaroscuro lighting satisfy the audience's generic expectations of 1960s Gothic horror (repetition), while the Eastman Color banner and Hammer's saturated, "rock and roll" style provide the difference "essential to the economy of the genre". The Universal logo works as a Barthesian referential code, rewarding cine-literate spectators with a call-back to Frankenstein and Dracula.

Representation

Gender & genre

  • The poster features no protagonists — only vampire antagonists and sacrificial prey.
  • The male vampire's one-handed grip demonstrates strength; the female victim's tight costume and exposed flesh offer male gaze pleasures and conform to sexist gender norms of the time.
  • But the female vampire is more powerful — sexualised yet central to the narrative, about to deliver the "vampire kiss" of the title. This is a binary opposite of the usual "woman as prey". The male victim is passive, throat exposed, effectively faceless — an "everyman" threatened by the newly empowered woman.
  • Reflecting the 1960s (women seeking greater equality — bell hooks), Hammer nods to female empowerment to attract female audiences, but frames feminism as a threat to patriarchal power. Genre shifts to reflect the social realities of when the film was made, not when it is set.

Theory you can apply

Van Zoonen, bell hooks, Gauntlett (representation & identity), plus Reception and Cultivation theory for historical vs contemporary audience response.

The female victim's tight costume and exposed flesh construct her through the male gaze, conforming to sexist norms (Van Zoonen). Yet the dominant female vampire inverts the usual "female as prey" convention — sexualised but central and powerful — reflecting 1960s anxieties about women's empowerment (bell hooks). Hammer thus uses genre to voice contemporary social change while exposing male fears, presenting feminism as a threat to patriarchal power.
