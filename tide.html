Advertising & Marketing — Tide

A 1950s Procter & Gamble washing-powder advert — a set text for Media Language, Representation and Audiences. Work through each section and answer the quick-checks to build your score.

The product & context

What is Tide?

A 1950s American print advert for Tide washing powder, made by Procter & Gamble. It is studied for Component 1, Section A (Media Language & Representation) and, for Section B, as an Audiences set text (Tide is Audience-only for Section B — no Industry).

Historical & cultural context

  • The post-WWII consumer boom of the 1950s brought new home technologies — vacuum cleaners, fridge-freezers and washing machines. Washing powder rode that wave.
  • Men were targeted for the post-war boom in the car industry; women for the technologies and products of the home.
  • Consumer culture was young, so 1950s print ads used far more copy than modern ads — audiences needed more information about unfamiliar new brands.

Media Language (VATL)

How a print advert works

A print advert combines text, image and organisation of the elements. Text anchors the image to add information; the image captures the concept (often selling more than the product); the layout builds a relationship between them to communicate a message.

  • Z-line layout — the eye scans left-to-right, top-to-bottom in a "Z", so key elements sit along that path.
  • Hard sell vs soft sell — hard sell is direct and obvious; soft sell is subtle. Tide is a hard sell.
  • Iconic / demonstrative representation — "see it working and it must be good".
  • Language techniques (CHILDE): Colloquialisms, Hyperbole, Imperatives, Lexis, Direct address, Emotive language.

Tide's language in action

Real copy from the advert: "No wonder you women buy more Tide than any other washday product!", "Tide's got what women want!", "World's cleanest wash! World's whitest wash!"

  • Direct address to "you women" builds a personal relationship and positions women as the buyers.
  • Hyperbole / superlatives ("World's cleanest… whitest") make exaggerated product claims.
  • Alliteration and a curved slogan add memorability.
  • Lévi-Strauss — meaning is built through binary oppositions (e.g. clean vs dirty, modern vs old-fashioned).

Representation

Gender in the 1950s

  • Tide constructs a stereotype of domestic perfection — women caring for the family and in servitude to the "man of the house", linked to modern speed and convenience.
  • Dress code: a stereotypical 1950s hairstyle (waves, curls, rolls made fashionable by film stars like Veronica Lake). A headband/scarf connotes she is focused on her work — arguably binary opposed to her full make-up.
  • This reflects the hegemonic patriarchal ideology of the era, presented as "just the way things are".

Applying theory

Liesbet Van Zoonen — a "depressing stability" in how women are framed: women belong to family and domestic life, men to the social world of politics and work; femininity is about care and nurturance, masculinity about efficiency and rationality. Tide illustrates this directly.

David Gauntlett — identity; bell hooks — feminism; Paul Gilroy — postcolonial theory can also be applied.

The alliterative, curved slogan and informal, direct language construct women as needing "simple", persuasive devices to be engaged — encoding the patriarchal ideology that women are less sophisticated. Van Zoonen's framing of women as belonging to domestic life is normalised as aspirational, reflecting the hegemony of 1950s gender roles.

Audience (Section B)

Targeting & positioning

The target audience is the 1950s housewife — likely newly married with a young family (clothing belonging to men and children on the washing line creates this connotation). Producers encode hegemonic ideologies of the era to normalise the content and suggest an aspirational lifestyle. Informal, direct language creates a personal relationship so the audience take a preferred reading — trusting the two women's positive endorsement in the comic-strip style.

  • Uses & Gratifications: the audience is constructed through women with whom they might personally identify — the comic-strip women "talking" about the product model a relatable, everyday scenario.
  • The endorsement from Good Housekeeping Magazine makes the magazine an Opinion Leader for the target audience, reinforcing the repeated assertion that Tide is the market-leading product.
  • The preferred reading is carried by reassuring lexical fields — "trust", "truly safe", "miracle", "nothing like" — signalling that despite being a "new" product, Tide solves the audience's domestic-chore needs.

How audiences are categorised

Advertisers classify audiences so they can be found and targeted:

  • Demographics — age, gender, ethnicity, location.
  • Socio-economic — grades A–E (based on occupation/income).
  • Psychographics / VALS — Values, Attitudes and Lifestyle. Young & Rubicam's Cross-Cultural Consumer Characteristics group people into types: Mainstreamers, Aspirers, Succeeders, Explorers, Reformers, Resigned, Strugglers. Tide targets the Mainstreamer housewife — the largest group, motivated by security, value and trusted brands.

Brand familiarity & the agency

  • The advert was made by the agency D'Arcy Masius Benton & Bowles (DMBB) — the same agency that created Coca-Cola's Santa image, so a trusted brand-builder.
  • The brand name is featured in the advert, and the same housewives sang the Tide jingle across TV and radio to build audience familiarity.

How context shapes interpretation

  • Post-war boom and the American Dream; washing-powder ads ran during daytime TV dramas — which is exactly why they became known as "soap operas".
  • After 14 years, rationing ended → prosperity and disposable income; the 1950s boom in TV sales widened audience reach.
  • A modern audience reads the same advert very differently (dated gender roles) — showing how historical context shapes audience interpretation.

Applying audience theory (Section B)

Stuart Hall — Reception: the 1950s housewife takes the preferred reading (accepting the aspirational, hegemonic message); a modern feminist takes an oppositional reading, rejecting it.

  • The indirect mode of address from the woman in the main image connotes that her relationship with the product is of prime importance — Tide has what she wants. This is the dominant/hegemonic encoding of the advert's primary message, intended to be received by "you women".
  • The direct mode of address of the images top-right and bottom-left link to the imperative "Remember" and personal pronouns ("your wash", "you can buy") — pulling the reader directly into the ad's claims.

George Gerbner — Cultivation: repeated domestic imagery of this kind cultivates the belief that "a woman's place is the home". The advert's own boast that Tide "goes into more American homes than any other washday product" is exactly the kind of repeated message Gerbner argues shapes audiences' view of reality over time.
