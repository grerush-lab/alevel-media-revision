The Bridge — Series 3, Episode 1 (2015)

The Eduqas Television in the Global Age set text (studied alongside Life on Mars): a Swedish–Danish Nordic noir crime drama, co-produced by Filmlance International and Nimbus Film, written by Hans Rosenfeldt. Product & contextThe set text at a glance

  • What: The Bridge (Bron/Broen) — a Nordic noir crime drama. The set text is Series 3, Episode 1, first shown in the UK on BBC Four, 21 November 2015. Four series ran 2011–2018.
  • The title/bridge: the Øresund Bridge links Malmö (Sweden) and Copenhagen (Denmark) — a literal setting and a metaphor for cross-border police collaboration (Series 1 opened with a body left on the bridge).
  • Series 3 plot: a prominent Danish gender campaigner, Helle Anker (owner of Copenhagen's first gender-neutral pre-school) is found murdered — her body theatrically staged at a Malmö construction site as a "nuclear family", deliberately left to be found. Swedish detective Saga Norén is paired with a new Danish colleague, Hanne — tense, because Saga had had her previous Danish partner jailed for murder.
  • Studied for: Media Language, Representation, Media Industries and Audiences, plus social/cultural, economic and political contexts.

Know these coldCharacter profiles

Episode 1 deliberately introduces a large cast who all seem potentially significant. Split them into the police investigation, the Helle Anker murder, and the wider web of suspects — and notice how many are made to look guilty. The murder is theatrically staged at a Malmö construction site: the killer wanted the body found, so the presentation is part of the mystery.

The police investigation

Saga Norén

Sofia Helin · Swedish detective

Malmö County Police. Brilliant, literal and blunt with no social filter (coded as autistic); follows the rulebook to the letter and drives a vintage Porsche 911. The central character and a feminist icon of Nordic noir.

Mostly challenges ✓Subverts the female stereotype — defined by intelligence and competence, not appearance or emotion, never sexualised (Van Zoonen). Counter-view: her autistic-coding can read as the "odd genius" trope.

Henrik Sabroe

Thure Lindhardt · Danish detective

First seen behaving in a sexually unconventional, emotionally detached way, so he initially looks like he might be a suspect. He becomes Saga's new partner and — unlike Hanne — is open to working with her.

Challenges ✓Challenges conventional ideas about relationships and masculinity, and is deliberately hard to categorise — suspect, dysfunctional man, or detective? The new male counterpart to Saga.

Hanne Thomsen

Kirsten Olesen · Danish detective

Assigned to Saga and openly hostile — she blames Saga for giving the evidence that jailed Martin. Rule-bound and more conservative (including on the gender-education debate). Injured early on.

Conforms ✗Conforms to conventional, rule-bound policing; a temporary obstacle who shows that Saga's actions have consequences in the police community.

Hans Pettersson

Dag Malmberg · Saga's boss

Saga's superior and a father figure — he accepts her rather than trying to change her, contrasting sharply with Hanne. But he has a complicated private life (his relationship with Lillian, an earlier family) and a hidden history.

BothChallenges ✓ the gruff-boss cliché as a supportive mentor — yet Aleksandr's grievance later complicates his image as the straightforwardly decent authority figure.

John

Rafael Pettersson · technical investigator

Works the forensic/technical side — the surveillance footage and cameras at the site, revealing that the killer deliberately interfered with the cameras.

Conforms ✗Conforms to the technical-investigator stock role — but shifts the question from "who hated Helle?" to "why was the murder staged like this?"

Lillian

Sarah Boberg · Danish police authority

Senior Danish officer and Hans's partner; she assigns Hanne to Saga and represents the Danish hierarchy needed for the cross-border investigation. More pragmatic and institutional than Hans.

BothConforms ✗ to institutional police authority, but challenges ✓ as a woman in senior command.

The Anker murder & the suspects

Helle Anker

the victim · gender theorist

A public intellectual and pioneer of gender-neutral education (she founded a gender-neutral preschool); a lesbian in a same-sex relationship who had received threats. Her body is theatrically staged at the construction site.

Challenges ✓Challenges traditional gender roles, biological determinism and the nuclear family. The staging makes the murder look ideological — but the episode wants us to distrust the obvious motive.

Morten Anker

Helle's son · ex-soldier

Helle's son from a previous relationship — a former soldier who served in Afghanistan, isolated, traumatised (PTSD) and living in a trailer. One of the episode's most obvious suspects.

Challenges ✓Set up as the "suspicious outsider", he actually challenges the lazy stereotype that trauma and mental illness = dangerousness.

Lise Friis Andersen

right-wing vlogger

A conservative/right-wing vlogger and Helle's ideological opposite — traditional values, publicly hostile to Helle's ideas. Linked to the construction site through her husband.

Conforms ✗Represents traditional values and is the obvious ideological enemy (clear motive) — which is exactly why she may be a red herring. Raises free-speech questions: criticism vs incitement.

Rikard

Lise's cleaner

The cleaner for Lise and her husband — polite on the surface, but fixated on Lise (he wears her jewellery), with disturbing interests (animals, surgical equipment).

BothPlays the "harmless-looking outsider" — suspicious enough to look like the killer, which is exactly why he works as a deliberate red herring.

Aleksandr

criminal · grievance against Hans

A criminal who believes Hans blackmailed him and used him as an informant. He opens a second storyline running parallel to the murder investigation.

BothChallenges ✓ our trust in Hans — forcing us to ask whether the "decent police boss" is as morally straightforward as he seems.

Framework 1 of 4Media Language — Nordic noir & genre

Nordic noir codes and conventions

  • Visual style: a muted, desaturated colour palette (greys, cold blues), low-key naturalistic lighting, bleak Scandinavian landscapes, gloomy weather and industrial/urban settings — connoting a bleak, morally grey world.
  • Pace & tone: slow-burn, understated, realist; a flawed, damaged detective; strong social/political critique beneath the crime.
  • Subtitles: Swedish and Danish dialogue with English subtitles — a marker of "quality"/foreign drama for its target audience.

Crime-genre & narrative theory

  • Neale — genre is repetition and difference: The Bridge uses the crime repertoire of elements (a body, investigation, interviews, the denouement) but differentiates through Saga and the Nordic setting.
  • Todorov — equilibrium → disruption (the murder) → resolution; but as Episode 1 of a series it is a flexi-narrative with many interwoven, unresolved strands and enigmas (Barthes).
  • Lévi-Strauss — binary oppositions structure the drama: Sweden vs Denmark, Saga vs Hanne (individualism vs institutional conformity), Saga vs Henrik (emotional detachment vs unconventional social confidence), Helle vs Lise (progressive change vs traditional conservatism), Hans vs Aleksandr (police authority vs criminal resentment), Helle vs Morten (public campaigner vs traumatised son), plus nuclear vs non-nuclear family and illusion vs reality.
  • A privileged spectator position at times lets the audience know more than the characters, building tension.

Framework 2 of 4Representation

Saga Norén — subverting the female stereotype

  • Van Zoonen — feminist theory: mainstream media often present women as spectacle / objects of the (male) gaze. Saga subverts this: she is defined by her intelligence and competence, not sexualised appearance, and is indifferent to how she is seen (she changes her top openly in the office).
  • She joins Sarah Lund (The Killing) and Lisbeth Salander as a feminist icon of Nordic noir — strong, complex women in a genre once dominated by men.
  • Neurodiversity: Saga is coded as autistic. This can be read as positive, nuanced representation of difference — or debated as reinforcing "otherness". Gauntlett — audiences use such figures as identity resources.
  • Gender & the victim: the murdered gender campaigner and gender-neutral pre-school foreground Scandinavian debates about gender — the show engages ideas linked to Butler (gender as constructed/performed).

Framework 3 of 4Media Industries — the global co-production

A Swedish–Danish co-production

  • Made by Filmlance International (Sweden) and Nimbus Film (Denmark), in co-production with the public broadcasters SVT (Sweden) and DR (Denmark), plus ZDF (Germany), NRK (Norway), Film i Skåne, the Copenhagen Film Fund and Nordvision.
  • Public funding: a €1 million grant from the Creative Europe MEDIA programme helped fund Series 3's high production values.
  • Why co-produce? TV drama budgets have soared; pooling money across partners spreads risk and raises production values so European drama can compete globally (like The Night Manager or The Crown). Hesmondhalgh — minimise risk / maximise audiences, here via co-production and the proven Nordic noir brand.

Public service, distribution & global reach

  • PSB: SVT and DR are national public-service broadcasters funded by a licence fee (like the BBC). In the UK it aired on BBC Four, whose remit is innovative, culturally enriching, foreign-language, subtitled content — following its Nordic noir strand (The Killing, Wallander).
  • Global reach: the first two series sold to around 160 countries; the format was remade as The Tunnel (Anglo-French) and a US version. This is TV as a global, not national, industry — with "narrowcasting" to niche audiences.
  • Curran & Seaton (ownership/PSB vs commercial power) and Livingstone & Lunt (regulation balancing citizens and consumers) both apply to this public-service, cross-border model.

Framework 4 of 4Audiences

Targeting & response

  • Target audience: an older, educated, ABC1 "quality TV" niche — comfortable with subtitles, complex narratives and social issues (BBC Four's audience; broadsheet readers). Reading subtitles marks an active, engaged audience with cultural capital.
  • Reach: UK Series 3 drew roughly 1.3–1.8 million viewers; in Sweden the premiere reached 1.5m — about 1 in 7 people. IMDb ~8.6/10, with a fairly even gender split.
  • Hall — reception: audiences produce preferred, negotiated or oppositional readings (e.g. embracing vs resisting the "issues"/feminist approach).
  • Jenkins — fandom: an engaged fan and critic community (Guardian recaps, online discussion) sustains the show; uses & gratifications include the puzzle, surveillance and personal identity.

ContextWhy Scandinavia? Social, cultural & economic

  • Social/cultural: Scandinavia is stereotyped as wealthy, liberal and equal — especially on gender equality (Sweden). Nordic noir gets its charge from the tension between that serene surface and dark undercurrents (e.g. the Palme assassination, Breivik). The gender-campaigner victim ties the crime directly to these values.
  • Economic: soaring drama budgets and risk aversion push broadcasters toward co-production and public/EU funding; a strong Nordic noir brand (after The Killing/Wallander) made the show a lower-risk global proposition.
  • Historical/industry: the shift from national to global television, "narrowcasting", box-sets, on-demand and subtitled foreign drama finding mainstream success.
