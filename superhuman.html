The product & context

What is Super. Human.?

Super. Human. is a 2020 audio-visual advert / trailer promoting the Tokyo 2020 Paralympic Games. It was devised by Channel 4's in-house creative agency 4Creative and produced by Serial Pictures and Somesuch, shot by Oscar-nominated cinematographer Bradford Young. It is the third C4 Paralympic campaign (after 2012 and 2016).

Studied for Component 1, Section A (Media Language & Representation) and Section B: Audiences — Audience-only, NOT Industries.

Charity/awareness conventions & compassion fatigue

Charity adverts aim for immediate, hard-hitting impact — often shocking (frequently black & white) images, emotive voice-over, personal narrative and close-ups, sombre music. But too much guilt causes compassion fatigue: audiences become numb and stop responding. Super. Human. deliberately challenges those conventions with an upbeat, "blood and guts" approach.

Media Language

Visual, audio & technical codes

  • Audio — the soundtrack "So You Want to Be a Boxer" from Bugsy Malone gives an upbeat, stirring feel with connotations of struggle and hard work paying off. Typical conventions (voice-over, dramatic music) appear briefly then shift to the alarm clock and daily reality. Grunts, groans and tape emphasise the battle of training.
  • Visual — iconography and settings link athletes to their sport (e.g. Ellie Simmonds putting on goggles in the pool), juxtaposed with everyday domestic scenes to show normality.
  • Technical — rapid editing; extreme close-ups of eyes (concentration); low-angle shots (power); slow-motion of the track-cycling crash with muted sound for dramatic effect.
  • Graphic: "To be a Paralympian there's got to be something wrong with you".

Applying theory to ML

Roland Barthes — the hermeneutic (enigma) code: the opening plants mysteries/unanswered questions that keep us watching.

Lévi-Strauss — binary oppositions: gruelling training vs celebration; dreamlike sequences (rich colour, music) vs harsh reality (ambient light, alarm); the graphic "something wrong with you" vs the positive images — a conceptual opposition the audience is invited to reflect on.

The on-screen graphic "To be a Paralympian there's got to be something wrong with you" creates a conceptual binary opposition (Lévi-Strauss) between the audience's preconception of disability and the reality of elite athletes. Set against Barthes' enigma code in the opening and the juxtaposition of sport with domestic struggle, media language is used to challenge myths around disability.

Representation

Representation of disability (and gender/ethnicity)

Choices construct representations that challenge accepted ideas about disability. The advert avoids the stereotype of Paralympians as victims to be pitied OR as "superhumans" to be viewed in awe. Instead it creates a realistic, "blood and guts" feel — celebrating the athletes as high-performance competitors in their own right, rather than people "overcoming" disability.

Applying theory to Representation

Stuart Hall — stereotyping reduces a group to a few traits and tends to occur where there are inequalities of power, with less powerful groups constructed as "other". Super. Human. resists this: the athletes are three-dimensional and distinct.

David Gauntlett — identity: the advert gives visible, positive recognition to disabled people, offering more diverse identities (though many disabilities are still not represented).

By presenting the athletes as ordinary sportsmen and women striving for a goal — distinct, three-dimensional individuals — the advert addresses Hall's assertion that excluded groups are constructed as "other". For Gauntlett, it broadens the range of identities available and lets disabled audiences see themselves represented positively, challenging the "disabled villain/victim" trope.

Audience (Section B)

Target audience & appeal

  • Broad demographic: Channel 4 viewers (largely 16–34), fans of sport, and reformers who want more inclusive representation — fitting C4's remit to appeal to "the tastes and interests of a culturally diverse society".
  • Personalisation — real human stories encourage the audience to identify with the athletes.
  • Uncomfortable close-ups (the popping blister; attaching a prosthetic blade) hold attention; humour (cartoon moments) lightens the mood.

Applying audience theory

Stuart Hall — Reception Theory: positioning plus preferred / negotiated / oppositional readings. Preferred: invest in the athletes and watch the Games. Negotiated: accept the message but feel distanced from sport. Oppositional: non-C4 viewers, older, uninterested in sport.

George Gerbner — Cultivation Theory: heavy TV exposure shapes our sense of reality. The advert can be said to challenge cultivation by offering a fresh, realistic view of disability that counters mainstream preconceptions.

Reach: C4's Paralympic coverage reached 20 million viewers — a third of the UK — 22% aged 16–34; the campaign reached 81% of the UK population.

Positioning & applied readings

Positioning — producers use images and sound to make us feel and think a particular way. Super. Human. positions us to admire the athletes as elite competitors and to watch the Games.

  • Preferred: invest in the athletes' narratives and watch the Paralympics — accepting Channel 4 (the encoder)'s intended meaning.
  • Negotiated: accept C4's legitimacy but feel distanced from sport, while still reconsidering misconceptions about disability.
  • Oppositional: non-C4 viewers, older audiences, or those uninterested in sport who reject the message.

What affects the reading?

Whether an audience decodes a preferred, negotiated or oppositional reading depends on their age, beliefs, culture, gender, life experience and even mood at the time of viewing.

Extra reach stat: beyond the 20m campaign reach, the Games drew 2.6 million viewers — Channel 4's biggest weekly share since 2016 and largest daytime share since 2012.
